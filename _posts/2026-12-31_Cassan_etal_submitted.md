Abstract
---

Owing to the growing number of spaceborne Earth observation missions, including
both optical and radar sensors, wide-area monitoring of flood events has become
increasingly feasible. Although several approaches can generate binary flood maps
(”water”/”non-water”), substantial uncertainties remain for specific land-cover classes,
particularly in vegetated or poorly observable areas. High-resolution digital elevation
models (DEMs) provide complementary topographic information that can improve flood
delineation and enable water-depth estimation across the entire flooded domain. This
study introduces the Flood Extent Estimation Technique (FEET), a method that
partitions flood regions into hydraulically coherent sub-basins within which the free
surface is assumed to remain constant. Similarly, river channels are discretised into
sections to estimate local free surface elevations. FEET accounts explicitly for areas
that are unobservable for a given satellite and completes the flood extent based on the
topography. The method was evaluated over three different floodplains for which
hydrodynamic models provide spatially and temporally continuous estimates of water
depth and extent. Comparisons between Sentinel-1 and SWOT observations and the
model outputs show strong agreement, particularly near flood peaks, including in areas
covered by vegetation. Furthermore, FEET provides velocity fields in the riverbeds, and
an initial assessment of discharge can be obtained using only a 1D steady-state
hydrodynamic model that can be readily implemented over large spatial domains.
